Six Stages Between the Antenna and the Receiver

Each stage exists to protect a downstream one. The order is not arbitrary: protection comes before amplification, and delay comes after the noise figure is set.

  1. Input protectionLimits the level that reaches the first amplifier. The burn-out rating of at least 10 W is the level the input survives without damage.
  2. Low-noise amplificationSets the noise figure for the whole chain. Published at ≤4 dB, rising to 4.5 dB across the temperature range.
  3. Switched delayAdds a 6-bit delay covering one wavelength at 12 GHz. Delay is applied after the noise figure is fixed so the switch loss does not sit in front of the LNA.
  4. Per-channel attenuationApplies amplitude taper at a 1 dB step over at least 31 dB. Attenuation states are used for tracking and for taper, not as a substitute for level control elsewhere.
  5. Gain and distributionBrings each channel to 40.5 ± 1.5 dB at the output and routes it to the connector field.
  6. Control interfaceCarries channel settings for delay and attenuation. The control word is agreed with the beam controller before layout.

What the numbers mean together

Gain, noise figure and tracking are specified together because they compete. Adding gain in front of the delay improves the noise figure but increases the power the switch must handle; increasing attenuation range reduces the output level available for the receiver. The gain budget is agreed as one set of values, not parameter by parameter.

Delay quantization matters as well: a 6-bit delay creates phase steps that appear as residual phase error after beam steering. The step size and the resulting error at the operating frequency are stated with the datasheet so the digitization can be judged against the array requirement.

RF Front-End Questions

Five questions asked before a multichannel front-end is specified.

Is the noise figure specified at ambient only?

No. The reference build publishes ≤4 dB at ambient and 4.5 dB across the operating temperature range. Both values are measured, because a receiver budget built only on the room-temperature figure will be optimistic at the top of the range.

Why is the delay referenced to 12 GHz?

One wavelength of delay is a physical length, so a delay range quoted in wavelengths needs a reference frequency. At 12 GHz one wavelength corresponds to a defined time delay; the same hardware produces a different number of wavelengths at another frequency. The reference is stated so the range can be converted correctly.

Does attenuation affect the noise figure?

Attenuation is applied after the low-noise stage in this architecture, so it reduces output level without directly setting the input noise figure. The exact effect depends on where the attenuation sits relative to the gain stages, and that placement is shown on the block diagram issued with the datasheet.

Can the channel count change from 13?

Yes. Thirteen channels is the reference build. Channel count changes the housing, the control board and the thermal path, so it is fixed before layout. The gain and tracking budgets are re-issued for the new count rather than scaled from this table.

What input level can it survive?

The published burn-out rating is at least 10 W at the input, with protection ahead of the first amplifier. The duration and frequency condition of that rating are defined with the datasheet; a continuous high level outside those conditions is reviewed separately.
